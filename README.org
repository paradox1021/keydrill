* keydrill

Drill Emacs key bindings in short sessions until they stick. Each
session introduces a few new moves, then recalls ones still being
drilled. At session start, keydrill looks up each command in the
buffer you launched from and drills *your* binding when the vanilla
default is no longer live.

Requires GNU Emacs 27.1 or later. Licensed under GPL-3.0-or-later.

** Status

v0.2.0. Not on MELPA yet; install with ~package-vc-install~ or from a
checkout. Re-check that the name ~keydrill~ is free on MELPA and GNU
ELPA on submit day.

The landmine matrix below has been walked in GUI Emacs and in
~emacs -nw~ on GNU Emacs 30.2, and playing real sessions by hand found
two defects the batch suite had missed (see CHANGELOG 0.1.0). Reports
from other terminals and other platforms are welcome.

** Usage

- ~M-x keydrill~ --- start a session on the curated Emacs deck. New
  moves arrive as intro cards showing the key; one correct press locks
  a move into the recall rotation, where you answer before you see
  anything. At most five new moves per session
  (~keydrill-max-new-per-session~). ~q~ leaves the session. A second
  ~C-g~ within one second also quits (~C-g~ itself is a drill answer
  when that is the card).

- ~M-x keydrill-benchmark~ --- one cold pass over the whole 85-move
  deck, live-resolved: no answers shown, no requeue, and *no writes to
  the progress store*. A measurement, not a lesson. Run it once before
  your first drill session --- that baseline is unrepeatable --- and
  weekly afterwards under the same conditions. Every answer is
  appended to the journal.

- ~M-x keydrill-observe-mode~ --- opt-in usage observer. *Off by
  default.* When on, it counts how commands are invoked (key, M-x,
  menu/mouse) by command and local ISO week, into the same local file.
  It does not record keystroke content, buffer text, or file names,
  and it ignores the minibuffer, isearch, and ordinary self-insertion.
  Enabling it the first time shows a buffer stating exactly that.

- ~M-x keydrill-report~ --- unused-binding gaps from the observer:
  commands you run via M-x, the menu, or the mouse that already have a
  key in your live keymap, ranked by count times keys saved. ~RET~ or
  a digit drills that row; ~a~ drills all listed moves.

- ~M-x keydrill-purge-data~ --- delete the local progress file after
  yes-or-no confirmation.

- ~M-x keydrill-journal-purge~ --- delete the answer journal after
  yes-or-no confirmation. Note that this command is not autoloaded: it
  is available once keydrill has been loaded in the session.

** The bar

A move is displayed as *learned* at three or more recall samples, at
least 90% first-try, and a last latency above 0 and strictly under
800 ms.

A session is at *graduation pace* when first-try accuracy is at least
95%, median first-try latency is above 0 and under 800 ms, and recall
attempts cover the full deck. The deck *graduates* after two distinct local
calendar days at that pace --- two days, not two sessions.

Intro cards never contribute to accuracy, latency, or graduation.
Teaching statistics and testing statistics are kept apart.

** Is this spaced repetition?

Not in the technical sense, and it is worth saying so before you find out
by reading the source.

keydrill has no interval algorithm. There is no per-move due date, no ease
factor, no SM-2. Within a session, recall is a shuffle of every move in the
drilling phase at open levels; a miss reveals the answer, waits for the
correct press, and queues one more copy before the session ends. Across
sessions the only scheduling pressure is a cap of five new moves per
session and a graduation gate that requires holding the bar on two separate
local days. If you arrived expecting [[https://orgmode.org/worg/org-contrib/org-drill.html][org-drill]] or [[https://github.com/abo-abo/pamparam][pamparam]] behaviour --- both of
which do implement real intervals, and both of which predate this --- that
is precisely what you are not getting.

Three reasons it is built this way.

*The bar is speed, not retrievability.* SM-2 and its descendants optimise
the probability that you can recall an item after N days. keydrill's pass
condition is a median under 800 ms. That is automaticity, not availability.
An interval schedule has no opinion about how fast an answer arrives, and
for a key chord "I remembered it, after four seconds" is a failure --- you
would have reached for the menu by then.

*Chords are procedural, and the motor-practice literature prescribes
within-session mixing.* The blocked-versus-random contextual interference
result (Shea & Morgan, 1979, replicated for forty years) is that mixed
practice feels worse during the session and retains and transfers better
afterwards. That is a within-session prescription, and shuffling recall
every session implements it directly. SM-2 grew out of declarative
vocabulary learning, which is a different memory system with different
consolidation behaviour.

*The deck is 85 moves, not 10,000.* Interval scheduling earns its
complexity when the review queue would otherwise be unmanageable. At this
size one session can cover the entire deck --- which is exactly what the
coverage condition for graduation demands --- so the problem that
scheduling exists to solve does not really arise.

None of that dismisses spacing itself. Distributed practice outlasting
massed practice at equal total time is about as settled as findings in this
area get (Cepeda et al., 2008). keydrill honours it at the *day* scale
rather than the *item* scale: short sessions by design, a five-new cap that
makes cramming pointless, and a graduation gate that cannot be satisfied in
one sitting no matter how well you perform.

Where the argument gets weaker, stated honestly:

- Once someone is carrying several decks, or a few hundred moves in the
  drilling phase, shuffling everything open is wasteful. A move you have
  held at 100% for three weeks costs the same session time as one you keep
  missing. The store already records last-seen and last latency per move,
  so weak-and-stale biasing is implementable without any new capture. It is
  on the roadmap, and it is the change most likely to make this section
  obsolete.
- I am not aware of a study that directly compares interval-scheduled
  against session-based practice for keyboard chords specifically. The
  reasoning above is an inference from adjacent literature --- motor
  learning, contextual interference, automaticity --- not a result. If you
  know of work that settles it, I would genuinely like to read it, and I
  will change the engine rather than the argument.

** Files and privacy

No account, no telemetry, no network. Two readable files live in
~user-emacs-directory~:

| File                   | Contents                                                     |
|------------------------+--------------------------------------------------------------|
| ~keydrill-data.eld~    | Per-move progress, per-deck records, and observer counts     |
| ~keydrill-journal.eld~ | One sexp per answered card: move, result, latency, timestamp |

Both are written with ~prin1~ and can be opened in a buffer. Set
~keydrill-journal-file~ to nil to disable journaling.

To confirm there is no network code in the package files:

#+begin_src sh
grep -rn "url-retrieve\|url\.el\|request" *.el
#+end_src

** Prior art

[[https://github.com/federicotdn/key-quiz][key-quiz]] (federicotdn) and keywiz already drill Emacs keys from a
fixed list. keydrill adds live-keymap resolution (when a deck command's
vanilla key is no longer live, the binding you actually use is drilled
instead), an opt-in usage-gap observer, and a cold benchmark with an
answer journal behind it.

Prompts were adapted from my web trainer.

** Terminal notes

Capture folds terminal and GUI spellings of the same binding: ~C-/~ ↔
~C-_~, ~C-SPC~ ↔ ~C-@~, ~TAB~ ↔ ~C-i~, and Escape-prefixed Meta chords
(~ESC x~ ↔ ~M-x~, ~ESC <~ ↔ ~M-<~, ~ESC C-\~ ↔ ~C-M-\~). Live lookup
skips menu-bar, mouse, and GUI names such as ~<open>~ (the File-open
event); ~find-file~ drills ~C-x C-f~, not that event.

The landmine matrix is human, not CI --- no batch test can observe how
a key actually arrives from a real terminal. It has been walked on GNU
Emacs 30.2 in GUI and in ~emacs -nw~ under Windows Terminal, and all
six chords behaved. If you use a different terminal, walk it once
before you trust a session there:

| Binding   | Why it is a landmine                           | GUI | tty |
|-----------+------------------------------------------------+-----+-----|
| ~C-g~     | Must score as an answer, not ~keyboard-quit~   |     |     |
| ~C-x C-c~ | Must not kill Emacs                            |     |     |
| ~C-/~     | Arrives as ~C-_~ in many terminals             |     |     |
| ~C-SPC~   | Arrives as ~C-@~ in many terminals             |     |     |
| ~M-<~     | Arrives as ~ESC <~                             |     |     |
| ~C-M-\~   | Arrives as ~ESC C-\~                           |     |     |

** Before you publish

These are not CI. Do them on a real machine:

1. Re-walk the landmine table above on any terminal you have not
   tested. ~C-M-\~ may only arrive as ~ESC C-\~; that still counts if
   the card scores a hit.
2. Play one session in ~emacs -Q~ and one in your real config (live
   keymap). Confirm that a command whose vanilla key you have unbound
   drills your binding instead.
3. Re-check the name ~keydrill~ on MELPA and GNU ELPA on submit day.
4. If an announcement quotes a move count: this deck is *85* vanilla
   GNU Emacs moves. Do not say 95. If cmdenter.app still says 95, fix
   that copy before any email that cites both.
5. Tag the release before announcing it. As of 0.2.0 the version
   header in ~keydrill.el~ must match the tag.

** How this was built

Worth stating plainly, because you would find out anyway and I would
rather you heard it from the README.

*The design is mine; most of the Elisp was written by AI coding agents in Cursor (Claude Opus 5, Composer 2.5, and Grok 4.6)
under my direction.* I wrote the build plan, the engine
specification, the deck, and the milestone breakdown before any code
existed, then drove an agent through it milestone by milestone, with
compile, lint, and the test suite gating each step. I reviewed the
output, specified the fixes, and ran every test.

*The tests are real, and the ones that mattered most were human.* The
suite is 122 ERT tests running in batch, plus byte-compilation with
warnings as errors, ~package-lint~, and ~checkdoc~. Before publishing
I also sat down and played real sessions in GUI Emacs and in
~emacs -nw~, which found two defects the suite had not: a "your
binding" note appearing on a stock keymap because ~where-is-internal~
happened to list ~C-<home>~ before ~M-<~, and a summary that printed
"0 ms" for a session in which nothing had actually been timed. Both
are the same kind of bug --- the package drawing a wrong /conclusion/
from correct inputs --- and neither is the kind a batch test tends to
ask about. If you find a third, that is the most useful bug report I
could get.

*I am not a longtime Emacs user.* I came to this from a web-based
shortcut trainer I built for other applications, and a suggestion that
the idea belonged inside Emacs rather than in a browser tab. Take that
as a warning about my instincts for Emacs convention and as an
invitation: conventions I have broken are worth telling me about. For
calibration, my own cold benchmark on the deck I built was *3 hits out
of 71 answered --- 4%, median 4.1 seconds*. I wrote a tool to fix a
problem I had.

*Relationship to my commercial work.* I run [[https://cmdenter.app][CmdEnter]], a paid web
trainer for shortcuts in other software, and I sell fluency training
engagements built on the same method. The prompts in this deck were
adapted from that trainer, which is why the Commentary credits it.
keydrill itself is GPL-3.0-or-later, has no network code, no account,
no telemetry, and no upsell, and it will stay that way --- it is not a
funnel and it is not crippled. If it were, the ~grep~ in the privacy
section would show it. This package is the method in public, where it
can be checked.
